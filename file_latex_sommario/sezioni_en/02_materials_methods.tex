\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Implementation, validation, and definition of
success}\label{sec:implementation-en}

For a base \(a\) coprime to the number \(N\) to be factored, the sequence
\(a^{x} \bmod N\) is periodic; its period, called the order \(r\), is the smallest
positive integer such that \(a^{r} \equiv 1 \pmod{N}\). The quantum part estimates it by
phase estimation and inverse QFT; the classical part derives the factors through rational
approximation and greatest common divisors, and verifies them.

The main instance is \(N = 15\), \(a = 7\), with eight control and four work qubits. Its
order is \(r = 4\), and the ideal control-register outcomes are 0, 64, 128, and 192. Each
measured execution, or \emph{shot}, returns a single outcome; many shots form the
histogram on which post-processing operates. Success means recovering non-trivial
factors, not reconstructing the exact order. Three of the four ideal peaks lead to it,
with an overall probability of 75\%. Classical verification is permissive, however: it
accepts 63 of the 256 values, so fully uniform outcomes would still succeed with
probability \(63/256 \simeq 24.61\%\). This reference depends on the instance and on the
post-processing, and does not measure the fidelity of the quantum state. It also shows
that correct factorizations can be obtained even when the distribution of the
control-register measurement outcomes has lost most of its ideal peak structure
\cite{en-smolin2013}.

A separate experiment evaluates Shor's sensitivity to gate errors by applying independent
Pauli errors after the compiled gates, excluding virtual RZ rotations. For each error
value, 20 replicates of 4,096 shots are run. The probability \(p_{g}\) describes physical
per-gate noise and is not directly comparable with the logical failure probability
\(p_{L}\), since Shor is not executed with a complete fault-tolerant compilation.

Circuit correctness is verified independently of factorization success, by checking
modular operators, restoration of the ancilla qubits, and agreement with the theoretical
distributions. Besides the main instance \(N = 15\), the instances \(N = 21\), \(a = 2\)
and \(N = 35\), \(a = 6\) are considered; ideally validated, they use Beauregard's
reversible arithmetic \cite{en-beauregard2002}. Only \(N = 21\) enters the exploratory
noisy AQFT tests, because for \(N = 35\) the available hardware resources did not allow
the simulation of an adequate Shor circuit. Qiskit/Aer, scikit-learn, Stim, and
PyMatching are used, recording versions, parameters, random seeds, and circuit
identifiers.

\subsection{Post-processing and classification}

Three strategies, sharing the same factor extractor and tie-breaking rule, operate on the
same histograms, one per quantum iteration, of 1,024 shots each. TOP-1 (Method 1) checks
only the most frequent outcome; TOP-4 checks up to four frequency-ranked candidates;
Method 2 applies TOP-4 only if a classifier predicts success for the dominant candidate.
The comparison between Method 2 and unfiltered TOP-4 is the ablation study, which
establishes whether the advantage comes from the learned model or simply from testing
more candidates.

Two illustrative scenarios, reference UC1 and stress case UC2, include depolarization,
thermal relaxation, and readout errors. For each scenario, 2,000 histograms with
parameters varied within \(\pm50\%\) of nominal are split 60\%/20\%/20\% into training,
selection, and test sets. Random forests, support vector machines (SVMs), and multilayer
perceptrons are compared on F1; the selected model is evaluated on 400 independent
histograms per scenario. The primary metric is the number of iterations needed to obtain
the factors; accuracy was treated as a secondary element in the study of the classifier,
while computation time was related to the available hardware resources. Thirty paired replicates per
scenario are used, with at most 50 iterations.

\subsection{Quantum error-correction strategy}

In quantum error correction, the information of one logical qubit is distributed across
several physical qubits; syndromes make it possible to detect errors without directly
measuring the logical state, and guide the decoder in choosing the correction.

Repetition and Steane codes serve as preliminary checks, while the main analysis concerns
memory circuits based on the surface code
\cite{en-steane1996,en-fowler2012,en-googleQECBelowThreshold2025}. An exploratory study
also compares the surface code with IBM's Gross code, a quantum low-density parity-check
(qLDPC) code \cite{en-bravyi2024}, with errors on data qubits only and perfect syndromes.

Performance is measured through the logical failure probability \(p_{L}\). The noise
model includes errors in gates, preparation, and measurement, with crosstalk between
adjacent qubits in some configurations.

For the surface code, the reference decoder is \emph{minimum-weight perfect matching}
(MWPM), which pairs the triggered syndromes by choosing the most probable error
configuration. MWPM is compared with a stand-alone neural network and with a hybrid strategy. BP+OSD
(\emph{belief propagation with ordered statistics decoding}) is an additional comparison
decoder and the decoder of the Gross code \cite{en-roffe2020}. The intervention threshold of the hybrid decoder is selected on the
validation set and kept fixed on the test set \cite{en-alphaqubit2024}.

\subsection{Optimization strategy through the approximate QFT}

The AQFT removes the small-angle controlled rotations of the QFT, reducing the number of
gates and the circuit depth at the cost of some precision \cite{en-barencoAQFT1996}.
Truncation of Shor's final inverse QFT for \(N = 15\) and truncation of the transforms
used in the modular arithmetic for \(N = 21\) are analyzed separately. The noise model is built from the gate infidelities and readout errors of the offline
FakeSherbrooke calibration, without adding \(T_{1}/T_{2}\) contributions and keeping
layouts and random seeds unchanged. The truncation degree is selected on a dedicated set
and evaluated on independent test data; the shots per configuration, 8,192 in the
\(N = 15\) pilot and 256 for \(N = 21\), are split equally between selection and test.
